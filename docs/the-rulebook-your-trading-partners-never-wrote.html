The Rulebook Your Trading Partners Never Wrote

Why EDI documents get rejected, why chargebacks arrive weeks later, and how a system can learn the rules your partners actually enforce

Mossrake Group, LLC · Learning the Game With No Rulebook, part 1

The shipment notice went out on time. The acknowledgment came back clean. The goods arrived. Six weeks later, a deduction appears on the remittance: noncompliant shipment notice.

Your team pulls the retailer’s implementation guide and checks the document line by line. By the guide, it was compliant. Someone opens a dispute. Someone else asks the one person on the team who has dealt with this retailer for years, and she remembers: this partner rejects shipment notices when the carton-level detail doesn’t match the way its warehouse expects to receive. It isn’t in the guide. It never was.

Every supplier to a large retailer has a version of this story. Most have dozens.

Every trading partner has two rulebooks

The first is the one they publish: the implementation guide, the vendor compliance manual, the routing guide. It is the rulebook someone intended.

The second is the one that decides whether you get paid in full: the rules the partner’s systems actually enforce. It includes everything the guide left out, everything that changed since the guide was last revised, and the interpretations that only show up when a document fails. It was never written down.

The gap between the two is where the money goes, and today the only record of the second rulebook lives in the heads of a few specialists who learned it one rejection and one chargeback at a time.

Where the unwritten rulebook costs you

Rejections

Documents fail with an error code or a terse message that says what failed, rarely why. Each one is a cycle of investigation, correction, and resubmission.

Onboarding

Bringing a new trading partner live means weeks of test cycles, discovering its real rules by trial and error before a single production order flows.

Drift

Partners revise their requirements, sometimes with notice and often without. Documents that passed last quarter start failing.

Deductions

The most expensive surprises arrive last: chargebacks for documents that were accepted, weeks after the fact, with the cause long out of sight.

And when the specialist who knows a partner leaves, the second rulebook leaves with her. The next person starts the education over.

Why your tools don’t solve it

EDI software is good at what it is built for. Translators and maps produce documents that are syntactically correct and encode the rules someone has already written down. Validation services check documents against the published guide. Deduction-management tools help you dispute chargebacks after they happen.

None of them can learn a rule that nobody has written down. That knowledge exists only in the partner’s behavior and in your specialists’ experience, and no tool on the market captures either one.

Learning the partner, the way your best specialist did

Your most experienced EDI specialist learned each partner’s real rulebook by working with it: sending documents, noticing what came back, forming a theory about why, and testing it on the next submission. Act-Learn, a system from Mossrake, does the same thing, systematically. Its main mode is working: given the job, it does the job, learns as it goes, and writes down what it learns.

  • It starts with the history you already have. The documents you sent, the acknowledgments and rejections that came back, and the deductions that followed. From that record it works out what the partner actually enforces.
  • It answers “why was this rejected?” with the rule that explains it and the earlier cases behind that rule.
  • It advises before you send. It checks an outgoing document against what it knows of that partner and says what it expects to happen.
  • Where there is a safe space, it can explore. A partner’s test environment is one. There, exploration mode can give the working sessions that follow a more complete world model, by trying one thing at a time to settle what the history leaves open.
  • It traces late surprises. When a chargeback arrives weeks later, it connects it back to the documents and conditions that caused it.
  • It starts from what your people know. Your specialists’ experience with a partner can be added on day one. It then affirms or corrects that knowledge by working.
  • Your analysts stay in charge. Everything it learns is readable. An analyst reviews each entry, corrects it, and locks the ones the team trusts.
  • It keeps learning. When a partner changes its requirements, the change shows up as surprises. The entry is reworded or ended, and the earlier wording is kept with its dates, so you can see what changed and when.

What it builds is a world model of each partner: a set of generalizations about what that partner enforces, each with the evidence behind it. In your terms, it is the partner’s actual rulebook, written down for the first time and kept current as the partner changes.

And it is yours. It is a document you hold and can read, not behavior buried in a vendor’s model. Change AI providers and it comes with you. What your team has learned about your partners stays your asset, to keep or to share as you decide.

What it did with a simulated retailer

To check what the system learns against what is actually true, we built a simulated retailer. It publishes a supplier guide. It also enforces eight rules the guide does not state. Four of them reject a document with a terse message. The other four accept the document and take a deduction twelve days later, and two of those share a single deduction code. The simulated supplier has a record of its past exchanges with the retailer and a test environment to send documents to. Nothing about the hidden rules was given to the system.

The supplier’s record, before. Working from the guide alone, the simulated supplier had sent 30 documents. Eleven were rejected. Of the 19 accepted, seven drew deductions later, twelve deductions in all.

The first job. We gave the system the same guide, the supplier’s record, and a task: ship and invoice eight open orders. It had no world model to start from. It read the record before sending anything. Every document it then sent was accepted, and none drew a deduction. At the end it wrote down what it had learned about the retailer along the way.

Exploring. The retailer’s test environment is a safe space to explore, so in a separate session we let the system use it, with no job to do. It read the record, then sent 27 documents of its own to test what it suspected. It established all eight unwritten rules, and nothing it wrote down was false.

What it established about the simulated retailer in exploration mode (its own entries, condensed)
A ship notice for a less-than-truckload order is rejected with pallet-level detail and accepted with carton-level. Truckload is accepted with either.Hidden rule: LTL shipments need carton-level detail · rejection message: “HL structure”
The PO number must be the zero-padded form shown on the order; an unpadded number is rejected.Hidden rule: the PO number must match exactly, leading zeros included · “PO reference”
Each line’s unit of measure must match the item’s unit on the order.Hidden rule: the unit of measure must match the order line exactly · “line N UOM”
An invoice is rejected when no accepted ship notice exists for its order, and accepted once one does.Hidden rule: no invoice before an accepted ship notice · “no receipt reference”
The ASN deduction is charged once if the notice is sent after its ship date, and once more if the ship date is three or more days before the delivery window opens. The two add up on one notice.Two hidden rules behind one deduction code · it separated them by shipping 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 7 days early and waiting for the deductions
The receiving deduction is charged when the carton count differs from the quantities divided by units per carton, with part cartons rounded up.Hidden rule: the carton count must follow from the quantities
The pricing deduction is charged when an invoice price is above the order price; the amount is the overcharge times the quantity.Hidden rule: an overcharge is accepted, then charged back

Seven entries covering the eight hidden rules. It also recorded what costs nothing, such as a short quantity or a second notice for the same order, which a specialist would want to know too.

The deduction entry shows what a safe space to explore is for. In the supplier’s record, every notice that was sent late had also been shipped early, so the two faults behind that one code looked like a single fault. No amount of reading can separate them, and ordinary work would rarely force them apart. Trying one thing at a time, somewhere a mistake costs nothing, did.

Working from that world model. In the sessions that followed exploration, the system was asked to ship and invoice eight open orders, starting from what it had learned. It did so three times. Each time it sent 16 documents, and none was rejected and none drew a deduction. Once, when its own notes did not account for an order, it asked a person rather than guess.

A change nobody announced. We then changed one of the retailer’s rules partway through a job. The first notice affected was refused. The system resent it unchanged to be sure, found the form the retailer now accepts, and finished all eight orders. At the end of the session it reworded the two entries that the refusal contradicted, kept the old wording with its dates, and listed both changes for a person to review. It recorded the refusal as an exception whose cause it had not yet pinned down, not as a change in the retailer’s rules. Its working advice, to use the form that is always accepted, was right.

One simulated retailer, built by us, with rules chosen by us. Documents were reduced to their fields rather than full X12 syntax, the simulator’s test environment charges deductions, which a real one would not, and each session was run once.

For EDI providers and integrators

If you onboard trading partners on behalf of many customers, the unwritten rulebook is your cost center. Every new partner connection is test cycles and specialist time, and what one engagement learns about a partner rarely reaches the next. A partner rulebook learned once, with its evidence, can be reused across every customer who trades with that partner and kept current as the partner changes. That turns onboarding knowledge into an asset instead of a repeated expense. Each partner’s rulebook stays that partner’s: nothing learned about one partner is assumed of another.

Not just a clever idea

The same approach, with nothing specific to EDI built into it, has been tested in very different settings.

In a simulated robot work cell, it was given a task and nothing else. It met two rules it had never been told in the middle of the job, adapted to each, and finished. In another series of sessions in the same cell, one of its rules was changed without notice. It met the change on its first action, finished the job another way, and replaced its entry with a more accurate one, keeping the old wording and flagging the change for a person.

An earlier version was tested on ARC-AGI-3, a benchmark of games that come with no instructions, no rules, and no stated goals. We gave it eight of the public games chosen at random. It won two outright, and of the 33 levels it completed, 30 were at or under human players’ efficiency. More telling for EDI: of 27 generalizations it formed that we checked against the games’ source code, 25 were correct, and every one was written down in plain language.
